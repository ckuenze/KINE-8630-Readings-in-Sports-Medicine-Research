What Is a "Count"? What Is a "Step"? How ActiGraph Actually Generates These Numbers

Every metric on this dashboard is built on top of two numbers the device hands you already processed: counts (the basis for Freedson, Freedson VM3, Troiano, and Evenson) and steps (the basis for the cadence metrics). Neither is a direct reading of acceleration — both come from a multi-step signal-processing pipeline inside the device and ActiLife software, most of which was undisclosed for decades.

Counts — band-pass filtered, thresholded, and summed acceleration. The full algorithm was proprietary for most of this field's history.
Raw tri-axial acceleration (in g) is first passed through a band-pass filter designed to isolate frequencies typical of human movement, rejecting both slow drift (gravity, posture, device shifting) and very high-frequency vibration. The exact passband has varied by device generation — the original AM7164 used 0.21–2.28 Hz; the GT3X/GT3X+/wGT3X-BT family commonly cites 0.25–2.50 Hz; ActiGraph's own 2022 technical disclosure gives the modern digital filter's peak gain at 0.759 Hz with −6 dB points at 0.212 and 2.148 Hz. The filtered signal is then rectified and thresholded — the original device counted each full 1/128th-of-range deflection above its 8-bit midpoint as one count; modern devices apply a comparable small-amplitude "dead-band" (on the order of 0.068 g) below which movement isn't counted at all — then summed over the recording epoch (commonly 1 s internally, re-aggregated to whatever epoch length you choose, 60 s here) to produce the final "counts" value. For most of the time these devices have existed, the complete algorithm was proprietary and known only through independent reverse-engineering; ActiGraph itself published the full technical details and an open-source reference implementation for the first time only in 2022 (Neishabouri et al.).
Steps — a separate peak-detection algorithm tuned to footstep timing, with real accuracy problems at slow walking speeds.
Steps come from a separate algorithm, not a byproduct of counts. It starts from the same band-pass filtered signal but looks for peaks matching the amplitude and timing pattern of a footstep on a single axis, rather than summing overall magnitude. Independent testing with controlled sinusoidal signals found the ActiLife step-detection algorithm is most sensitive to movement around 0.7–0.8 Hz and requires a minimum acceleration amplitude of roughly 0.07 g to register a step — broadly similar to, but not identical to, the counts algorithm's own threshold, since the two are tuned for different purposes. This has real, documented accuracy consequences: step detection is known to under-count substantially at slow walking speeds (well under 50% agreement with observed steps at speeds at or below ~1.0 m/s in some validation studies, improving sharply above that), and accuracy depends on wear location (hip tends to be more reliable than wrist, where incidental arm movement creates false positives and negatives). ActiGraph offers an optional "Low Frequency Extension" filter specifically to improve sensitivity at slow speeds, at some cost to specificity.
The "plateau effect" — counts can level off or even drop at the highest activity intensities.
Because the band-pass filter's upper cutoff rejects the higher movement frequencies that occur during fast running, counts can level off or even decrease at the highest activity intensities — meaning counts-based cutpoints may be least reliable exactly where distinguishing vigorous from very-vigorous activity matters most.
Counts depend on raw sampling rate, not just the activity itself — the same aliasing issue from earlier this semester.
Controlled testing has shown the same physical movement recorded at different raw sampling rates can produce meaningfully different final count values (hundreds to over a thousand counts/min difference between 30 Hz and 100 Hz during running), due to imperfect anti-aliasing in the resampling step. The same movement, same device, different sampling rate: different counts.
